Day 29: the Kilnworth method

A potters’ village has become the proving ground for how we want to build Sprawl: start with a place worth exploring, turn its shapes into a world you can walk through, and keep comparing the result with the original ambition.

20 October 2026

This is an in-game capture of Kilnworth. The sheds, kilns, jars, gardens and bridge are drawn by the game’s renderer from a generated town plan. The reference painting guides the work; the scene you walk through is built from geometry.

A small village, a big change

Kilnworth began with a very specific place: cottages around a working yard, potters’ sheds stepping back beneath bottle kilns, a clay pit up a haul track, and a mill beside a falling stream. Its trade explains its materials. Potters fire tiles, so their roofs wear those tiles. The yard needs room for work, customers and carts.

Those relationships matter as much as the individual buildings. A convincing kiln beside an arbitrary rectangle of houses still loses the village. Kilnworth pushed us to treat composition, land use, light and movement as one problem.

We call that working approach the Kilnworth method. It is becoming a central part of Sprawl’s visual development.

What the method actually means

  • Read the place. Identify its landmarks, working routes, building groups, changes in height and important views. Ask why each part belongs there.
  • Build those relationships into rules. Give the yard, kilns, sheds, pit and water their own roles in a generated plan. Preserve room to work and walk.
  • Render a shared scene. Build three-dimensional shapes from that plan, then trace them into the game’s pixel view with consistent materials, sunlight, soft shadows and light in sheltered spaces.
  • Keep the place playable. Check doors, bridge crossings, ramps and interiors. Retain depth so a person can disappear behind a kiln and appear inside a cutaway house.
  • Compare, criticise and repeat. Match the reference’s framing, inspect close-ups, rank the largest differences and make another pass. Then try another seed.

That final step is essential. A successful build says that the code runs. The picture still has to earn its place.

The light belongs to the whole town

Kilnworth’s renderer gives materials colour ramps drawn from the reference, then uses the scene’s light to choose their tones. Brick, slate, timber, clay and canvas each keep their own character in sun and shade. Shadows and the darkening of corners help the sheds sit on the ground and the kilns rise behind them.

The result is a depth-bearing pixel image. The game uses that depth when drawing people, and replaces a house with its cutaway when you enter. The rendered town and its cutaways are cached for later visits. First-time preparation still has a cost, and reducing it is part of the work ahead.

The screenshot is evidence of this particular town at a particular stage. It does not mean every town or biome has reached the same standard.

A method for procedural places

Kilnworth is one deliberately pinned brief for the potters’ village generator. Change the seed and the same rules produce another potters’ town. Change the brief and the composition can change with it.

Separate random streams help keep changes local: adding wares need not rearrange the trees. The useful discovery is a set of relationships that can survive variation: kilns behind the working range, access through the yard, a pit with a usable ramp, and a mill that belongs to its water.

That is how a carefully composed reference can help procedural generation. The goal is to teach the generator what makes a place feel intentional, then test whether it holds together beyond the showcase seed.

Taking it beyond Kilnworth

The approach is being carried into the timber town, coaching town, Plumtree and Oakwell, with broader regional work around them. Their different structures need their own treatment: occupied carriage arches, balconies, terraces, stairs and joined buildings all have to look right and remain usable.

The same discipline also guides the biome work: study the actual tree shapes, ground cover, water, terrain and lighting; compare the result; revise the largest misses. Biomes need their own generation and streaming solutions, so the town renderer alone cannot finish them.

We want settlements shaped by terrain, work and history: winding approaches, irregular neighbourhoods, changes in elevation and useful open spaces. The wider rollout is still being developed and reviewed.

What still needs to improve

Kilnworth itself remains open to criticism. Water and mill details, the openness and life of the sheds, material texture, figure readability and preparation time have all needed further attention. Other towns need matched views and real walks through their occupied levels before we can judge the transfer.

The method gives us a repeatable way to demand more from the result. A place should look inviting from a distance, make sense when you arrive, and reward you for walking into it. Kilnworth is where that ambition started becoming a practical way of working.
