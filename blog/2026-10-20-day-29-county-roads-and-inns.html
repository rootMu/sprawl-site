Day 29: roads worth travelling, inns worth finding

The towns are starting to belong to a larger place. County planning, merchant journeys, roadside hospitality and geographical knowledge are being developed together, so the space between settlements can become part of the adventure.

20 October 2026

A county around the towns

The county planning work brings eleven expanded towns and the city of Marlbury into an approximately 85.5-square-kilometre region spanning grassland, temperate forest and birch woodland. Marlshire and Marlbury are working names.

The expansion adds land, streets, neighbourhoods and houses while preserving individual building dimensions. The fitted plan records nineteen road links and fifteen planned roadside sites, with approaches checked against the settlement geometry.

Those measurements describe a development plan, using a provisional world-unit convention. They are not a claim that the entire county is finished or continuously playable. Connecting terrain, scenery, collision and streaming remains a separate task, with the Oakwell-to-Marlbury route providing a useful first connection to prove.

The journey should have a life of its own

The countryside direction is a meandering road through flowering meadows, scattered trees and woodland, with places to stop and reasons to leave the road. Coaching inns, shrines and hunting cabins can give a journey landmarks and smaller stories.

Merchant work is being connected to the county’s existing roads and settlement identities. The ambition is a trading life that continues around the player: towns making and needing different goods, commissions travelling with merchants, and disrupted journeys creating work for guards and adventurers.

The route’s appearance still needs review. A connected line on a planning map cannot tell us whether a bend feels natural or a birch wood looks like birch.

The Bent Willow

The roadside inn preview now gives the idea a more concrete home. Mara shares news and offers a funded recovery job. Lark has songs, Brindle has tales, and hospitality work includes finite-stock meals and a real upstairs bed.

The expanded inn work adds staff and visiting performers with stable identities and attendance tied to the county clock. Its native review exercised conversations, meals, physical stair walking, upstairs rest, saving and a scheduled minstrel visit. These are development-preview results, rather than a finished network of populated inns.

Generated inn rules now account for the kind of inn, its traffic, prosperity and facilities. Keepers and cooks form the core; beds and stables bring their own workers. Bards are common, while storytellers and minstrels have separate visit patterns and repertoires.

The next challenge is making those inns belong to their surroundings. A generated building still needs a dry plot, a fit to the terrain and a real approach from the road. Staff commuting, physical deliveries and a full hospitality economy remain future work.

Learning the land

The exploration foundation distinguishes geographical knowledge from a map drawn from it. Observation can improve what is known, while a map draft records a bounded version of that knowledge and the traveller’s annotations.

That gives cartography somewhere meaningful to go: maps that improve through exploration and practice, with unknown country left unknown. Drawing materials, inventory-backed map items, inherited mistakes and the finished parchment interface still need further work.

Keeping the visual promise

The Kilnworth method gives this larger effort a practical discipline: build from a clear visual ambition, preserve playable relationships, compare the actual result and keep revising. The county needs that scrutiny at every scale, from a town’s skyline to the flowers beside a lane.

There is now more structure behind the adventure. The work ahead is to make travelling through it feel as rich as the plan suggests.
